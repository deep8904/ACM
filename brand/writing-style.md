# Writing Style

## 1. Voice

The desired voice is clear, practical, technically informed, and personally reasoned. It should sound like a real writer who understands software, design, gaming, and creator technology.

The writing should not imitate Deep's casual text-message spelling. The objective is a polished version of his natural communication style: direct, curious, simple, and human.

## 2. Tone

- Confident when evidence is strong.
- Explicitly uncertain when evidence is incomplete.
- Interested but not overexcited.
- Critical without becoming cynical.
- Professional without sounding corporate.
- Conversational without filler.

## 3. Sentence and paragraph style

- Mostly short and medium-length sentences.
- One clear idea per paragraph.
- Short paragraphs for online readability.
- Use longer sentences only when the relationship between ideas requires it.
- Prefer active voice.
- Avoid repetitive sentence openings.
- Use lists only when they improve comparison or scanning.

## 4. Article opening

Open with one of:

- The specific change and its consequence.
- A tension or unanswered question.
- A practical reader problem.
- A surprising but supported contrast.

Do not begin with broad statements about a fast-changing world.

Bad:

> In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, innovation is transforming how we work.

Better:

> Figma's latest update removes one of the slowest parts of design handoff, but it does not solve the trust problem between designers and generated code.

## 5. Explanation pattern

When explaining a technical topic:

1. State the change simply.
2. Give the minimum necessary context.
3. Explain how it works.
4. Show a practical example.
5. Explain who benefits.
6. Identify tradeoffs.
7. Give a reasoned conclusion.

## 6. Opinion style

Opinions must be tied to evidence or a stated value.

Use:

- "The useful part is..."
- "The limitation is..."
- "For small teams, this matters because..."
- "I would wait if..."
- "Based on the available testing..."

Avoid pretending a preference is universal.

## 7. Product content

Never imply hands-on use unless Deep actually tested the product.

Use labels such as:

- Source-based review.
- Buying analysis.
- First-look analysis.
- Specification comparison.

Discuss:

- Intended user.
- Build and usability.
- Performance evidence.
- Software experience.
- Compatibility.
- Warranty and support.
- Price relative to alternatives.
- Known complaints.
- Who should skip it.

## 8. Forbidden or overused language

Avoid:

- game-changing
- revolutionary
- groundbreaking
- cutting-edge
- next-level
- seamlessly
- unlock
- unleash
- delve
- leverage
- robust solution
- in today's fast-paced world
- ever-evolving landscape
- it's important to note
- without further ado
- the future is here
- this changes everything
- whether you're a beginner or a professional
- at the end of the day

Avoid forced contrasts such as "It's not just X, it's Y" unless genuinely useful.

## 9. Formatting

- Use descriptive headings.
- Avoid headings every two paragraphs.
- Keep headings informative, not clever.
- Use code blocks for actual code.
- Use tables for structured comparisons only.
- Include a concise source section when appropriate.
- Do not overuse bold text.

## 10. Conclusion

The conclusion should answer the practical question created by the article. It may include:

- Who should act.
- Who should wait.
- What evidence is still missing.
- What to watch next.

Do not repeat the entire article or end with generic inspiration.
